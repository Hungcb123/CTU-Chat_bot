\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study developed and evaluated \system{}, a supervisor-routed multi-agent RAG framework for heterogeneous university counseling. Three findings answer the research questions. For RQ1, at 51 tools the routed specialist exceeds the global Top-10 single-agent baseline by 9.3 percentage points in decision-level end-to-end pass rate (95\% CI $[+2.0,+17.3]$), but uses 70.8\% more input tokens and $2.03\times$ the latency. Relative to full-registry single-agent execution, it uses 52.4\% fewer input tokens, while its 4.7-point end-to-end difference has a CI containing zero. The evidence therefore supports a fixed-registry advantage over Top-10 at 51 tools, not an asymptotic scaling-rate advantage. For RQ2, the full heterogeneous configuration exceeds the three retrieval baselines and the no-reranker ablation on all eight metrics, including a 0.1018 Faithfulness difference from the latter (95\% CI $[+0.0294,+0.1762]$); comparisons with the remaining ablations are mixed and support component-specific associations rather than uniform effects. For RQ3, the complete retrieval configuration records the highest aggregate scores and matches or exceeds the reranked baseline across all complexity tiers, although its bundled mechanisms prevent attribution to any single component.

Taken together, these findings are consistent with the interpretation that representation-specific evidence paths---graph traversal for curricula, deterministic calculation for statutory fees, and hybrid retrieval for narrative regulations---can accommodate heterogeneous institutional knowledge under the evaluated conditions. The study contributes an implemented architecture and an evaluation protocol that expose both reliability gains and operational costs. In deployment, specialist routing is supported when tool ambiguity and bounded evidence access are priorities, whereas a Top-$k$ single-agent gate remains preferable when latency and input-token cost dominate. These implications remain limited by the offline, single-institution benchmark, automated evaluation, implementation-level failures, and dependence on upstream classification and route repair.

Future work should (1)~reduce routing latency through parallel candidate dispatch and prompt compression; (2)~test multi-institutional and bilingual benchmarks with naturally evolving tool registries; and (3)~conduct human-in-the-loop field trials of student trust, advisory efficacy, and practical usability.
